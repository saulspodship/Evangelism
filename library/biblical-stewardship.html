GUIDE 06 — FAITHFULNESS

Biblical stewardship:
the ground under the work.

Behind every good leadership and evangelism ministry lies a great stewardship quality. This guide covers what we have been entrusted with, why local resources run scarce, and how a church funds its own mission.

  • AuthorArthur Wilson
  • ThemeStewardship, leadership, and funding mission
  • ReadingAbout 8 minutes
  • OriginalBiblical Stewardship paper (PDF)
THE THESIS

Behind every good leadership and evangelism ministry lies a great stewardship quality. Scripture speaks of stewarding our time, money, talents, and the gospel message itself. Psalm 24:1 and 1 Peter 4:10 say that everything we have is from God and for God. We are managers of it, not owners, called to use God's gifts for His glory and the good of others.

Stewardship is not just about money. It is a holistic lifestyle of faithful management and intentional obedience.

01

Stewardship behind effective leadership

Leadership as service, not control

A steward-leader is defined by what they do with what they have been given.

  • Stewardship defines leadership as service, not control. “Whoever wants to become great among you must be your servant” (Matthew 20:26).
  • Leaders steward influence, authority, and people with humility and accountability to God.
  • A steward-leader acts with accountability (Romans 14:12), faithfulness (1 Corinthians 4:2), and wisdom (Proverbs 4:7).
  • Steward-leaders align their decisions with God's purposes—kingdom advancement, community transformation, holiness, and justice—and not with personal interests.
Matthew 20:26 Romans 14:12 1 Corinthians 4:2 Proverbs 4:7
02

Stewardship behind effective evangelism

The gospel is entrusted, not owned

  • All believers are stewards of the gospel message, entrusted by Jesus in Matthew 28:19–20 and described by Paul in 1 Corinthians 4:1.
  • Stewardship motivates love for the lost, sacrificial service, and grace-filled witness.
  • Stewardship ensures integrity in evangelism: honesty in the message, purity in methods, and faithfulness in follow-up.
Matthew 28:19–20 1 Corinthians 4:1
03

Why indigenous resources are scarce

Ten reasons, named honestly

“Indigenous resources” means more than local funding—it includes local people, materials, knowledge, and leadership. The handout names ten limiting factors across structural, cultural, educational, economic, and organisational lines.

01

Dependence on external support

Ministries may rely heavily on foreign missionaries, funding, and materials, which reduces the incentive to develop local alternatives. External partners may unintentionally create a dependency mindset, where local communities feel they cannot lead or resource their own ministry.

02

Lack of local training and discipleship structures

Indigenous leaders often lack formal theological education or leadership training due to limited access or cost. Churches may not have systems in place for ongoing mentorship and skill development.

03

Cultural barriers

Western models of ministry are sometimes imported without adaptation, making local people feel that their culture, language, or practices are inadequate. Traditional knowledge and cultural expressions of worship may be undervalued or discouraged.

04

Economic constraints

Local Christian communities in some regions face poverty, instability, or a lack of sustainable income sources. Churches may not have enough local funds to invest in training, facilities, or resource development.

05

Limited access to materials and technology

Indigenous-language Bibles, literature, media, and training materials may be scarce or outdated. In remote areas there may be limited printing, internet, or distribution infrastructure.

06

Leadership challenges

Existing leaders may unintentionally centralise authority, preventing younger or emerging leaders from developing. A lack of succession planning can hinder long-term growth of indigenous ministry capability.

07

Social and political barriers

In some contexts indigenous communities face marginalisation, discrimination, or political restrictions on religious activity, limiting the pool of potential ministry workers.

08

Mission strategies that do not prioritise local ownership

Some mission models focus on short-term projects rather than long-term empowerment, and prioritise external expansion over internal capacity building.

09

Fear of failure or low confidence

Indigenous believers may feel unprepared, or fear they cannot meet expectations set by outside models, and so defer leadership to outsiders who appear more qualified.

10

Lack of contextualisation

Ministry materials may not be adapted to the local language, culture, worldview, or learning style. When a community does not see itself reflected in the resources, they may not engage deeply or take ownership.

04

The dangers of dependence on foreign funds

What happens when the money comes from elsewhere

The handout does not dismiss generous giving across borders. It insists that Paul taught both a self-support model and generous giving toward God's work—he made tents to support himself (Acts 18:3) and also received support from churches (1 Thessalonians 2:9; 2 Thessalonians 3:7–9). What it warns against is dependence.

01

Loss of local ownership and initiative

When a church relies heavily on foreign funding, local believers may become passive recipients rather than active contributors. Such dependence can suppress local creativity and leadership, because decisions may be shaped more by donor priorities than by local context.

02

Paternalism and power imbalances

Foreign support can lead to a paternalistic relationship, where donors or agencies exert undue influence over the vision, doctrine, and decision-making of the local church. This can undermine the dignity of local leaders and create a sense that they are not fully trusted to lead.

03

Theological and intellectual stagnation

Relying completely on foreign knowledge discourages independent thinking and creates theological poverty in the local community. Leaders focused on keeping foreign funds coming may neglect rigorous theological education or intellectual growth.

04

Sustainability risk

If core ministry functions such as salaries and operational costs are primarily funded from abroad, the church may collapse if that funding is reduced or withdrawn. Long-term reliance prevents the development of local financial systems—tithing, a giving culture, and income-generating ministries.

05

Erosion of local credibility

Local communities may view the church as a foreign entity rather than a genuinely indigenous expression of faith. The use of foreign funds can also raise suspicions, such as accusations of buying converts or creating a class of pastors paid by external donors.

06

Moral and motivational distortion

Leaders who receive salaries from abroad may work to secure income instead of depending on God. It causes jealousy, competition, or even corruption among local leaders.

07

Risk to integrity and accountability

Handling foreign money can bring risks of corruption, especially where financial management systems and skills are weak.

08

Distorted mission priorities

Ministry work may become donor-driven—buildings or events—or fundraising-driven and project-based, instead of addressing deeper, context-specific needs.

09

Dignity and self-worth issues

Dependence on foreign aid weakens local believers' ability to sustain their work and can prevent them from using local economic ways such as loans, investments, or other self-support systems.

10

Missionary exit challenges

If a mission agency or foreign donors withdraw, there may be no infrastructure for the local church to continue.

Acts 18:3 1 Thessalonians 2:9 1 Thessalonians 4:11–12 2 Thessalonians 3:7–9
05

Biblical principles of stewardship

Six things Scripture settles

  1. Everything belongs to God. We are stewards of it, not owners—“Beware lest you say in your heart, ‘My power and the might of my hand have gotten me this wealth’” (Deuteronomy 8:17–18); “You did not choose me, but I chose you and appointed you that you should go and bear fruit” (John 15:16).
  2. Stewardship is not only money. It includes our time, talents, relationships, testimony, and the created world (Titus 1:7–8; Ephesians 5:15–17).
  3. There is accountability to God for the use of everything entrusted to us, for the building up of the church body—“Well done, good and faithful servant” (Matthew 25:23); “each of us will give an account of himself to God” (Romans 14:12); “If then you have not been faithful in the unrighteous wealth, who will entrust to you the true riches?” (Luke 16:11).
  4. Priority giving to God of the first fruits of our lives and resources—“Honour the Lord with your wealth, with the first fruits of all your produce” (Proverbs 3:9); “as each has received a gift, use it to serve one another, as good stewards of God's varied grace” (1 Peter 4:10–11).
  5. Cheerful giving and the heart's attitude toward our treasures (Matthew 6:21; 2 Corinthians 9:7): “Whatever you do, work heartily, as for the Lord and not for men” (Colossians 3:23–24).
  6. Trust in God's provision so that people can give freely because God will provide for their needs—“All your works shall give thanks to you, O Lord… to make known to the children of man your mighty deeds” (Psalm 145:10–12).
Deuteronomy 8:17–18 John 15:16 Titus 1:7–8 Ephesians 5:15–17 Matthew 25:23 Luke 16:11 Proverbs 3:9 1 Peter 4:10–11 Psalm 145:10–12
06

The blessings of tithing and second-mile giving

First fruits, then everything

Malachi 3:10 sets out the invitation: “Bring the full tithe into the storehouse… and thereby put me to the test, says the Lord of hosts, if I will not open the windows of heaven for you and pour down for you a blessing until there is no more need.”

In the Old Testament, the tithe was a tenth of produce, livestock, and increase brought to God. The New Testament expands the concept into heart-based giving, with examples of giving everything to God rather than just the tenth part.

Paul does not stop at a set amount. In 2 Corinthians 9:6–11 he speaks of going above and beyond, giving in every way, so that the church grows spiritually, emotionally, and financially: “whoever sows sparingly will also reap sparingly… God loves a cheerful giver.”

How the blessings are received

  • Through discipleship and biblical training on stewardship, people learn to serve God with their resources (Proverbs 3:9–10; 1 Corinthians 4:1–2; the parable of the talents).
  • Encourage people to make a stewardship plan that honours God—deciding a portion for giving, saving, and spending—and to give the first fruits, prioritising the giving budget first rather than giving from the leftovers.
  • Give sacrificially, not only what is comfortable. Count the cost in time, effort, and money.
Malachi 3:10 2 Corinthians 9:6–11 Proverbs 3:9–10 1 Corinthians 4:1–2
07

Why teach biblical stewardship

Four motivations

  • Giving is not just an expense in someone's budget; it is a spiritual act.
  • Empowering local believers to steward their resources builds church sustainability—less dependent on foreign mission support, more rooted in the local community.
  • It builds disciples: generosity shapes character, faith, and spiritual maturity.
  • It creates a culture where mission, mercy, and community care are financed and owned by the local church, not dependent on outsiders.
08

Developing indigenous resources

Ten ways to build local capacity

This is the practical heart of the handout: what a church or ministry can actually do to become locally rooted and locally resourced.

01

Contextual leadership training

Biblical and theological training tailored to the local culture, with mentoring and coaching programmes.

02

Self-governance and indigenous ownership

New churches and ministries built on self-governing, self-propagating, and self-supporting principles, empowering local believers with real authority to plan, decide, and lead.

03

Sustainable financial models

Develop budgeting practices where local churches contribute to their own costs, invest in locally appropriate income-generating projects such as farming or small enterprises, and train leaders in resource mobilisation rather than relying solely on external donors.

04

Contextualised ministry models

Use local architectural styles, materials, and structures; encourage local believers to exhibit their own expression of church development; and promote indigenous-led movements such as church planting and disciple-making movements.

05

Partnerships and networking

Local partnerships among churches and mission organisations to empower local leaders, with mutual support, collaboration, and shared resources for ministry funding.

06

Cultural trust and relevance

Respect and integrate local cultural practices, language, and worldview into how the gospel is communicated and lived out. Indigenous leaders can contextualise teaching in culturally meaningful ways, which builds trust within communities—local leaders are often more effective at evangelism, discipleship, and social transformation.

07

Long-term vision and succession planning

An ongoing, indigenous leadership-development system that trains and raises more leaders through retreats, conferences, and seminars.

08

Community development as ministry

Integrate holistic development—economic, social, environmental—into ministry so the church contributes tangibly to local well-being, which strengthens its legitimacy and sustainability. Community-led models identify needs and resources with church support.

09

Accountability and good governance

Build accountability structures in local church leadership—financial, spiritual, organisational—so the use of local resources is transparent and trust is maintained. Strengthen governance in local theological institutions.

10

Measure, adapt, and scale

Encourage local leaders to reflect on what is working and what is not, adapt strategies to local realities, and scale successful models, using missiological strategies such as church-planting movements to assess how well indigenous churches are reproducing.

09

Goals for giving and fundraising in evangelism

Ten disciplines for leaders

The handout closes with a leader's checklist for raising funds for evangelistic work without losing the plot. Every item assumes the money serves the mission, never the reverse.

01

Align goals with vision and mission

Make sure your financial goals are directly tied to your ministry's vision, especially evangelism aims: what do you want to accomplish with the funds? Communicate this vision clearly to your supporters—people give more generously when they understand why their money matters.

02

Set both short-term and long-term goals

Short-term: what you need for an upcoming project. Long-term: sustainable funding for ongoing ministry, such as a monthly support goal or a reserve fund. Use aspirational yet realistic numbers, with a minimum goal and a challenge goal.

03

Understand and segment your givers

Identify different donor personas—local church members, regular supporters, occasional givers, larger donors—and set tailored goals for how much you expect from each, how often, and through which channel.

04

Use clear, measurable metrics

Define concrete metrics: amount raised, number of monthly givers, donor retention rate. Track progress regularly and transparently, and share updates so supporters can see how close you are to the goal.

05

Make giving accessible and easy

Provide multiple giving options—online giving, text-to-give, in-service giving, recurring donations. Promote recurring giving, which stabilises income, and label different funds so givers can decide where to give.

06

Educate and empower your congregation

Teach biblical stewardship as part of discipleship so giving is spiritual rather than transactional. Offer workshops on budgeting, financial planning, and generosity, and regularly show how giving contributes to evangelistic impact.

07

Build a fundraising team

Assemble a small team for planning, communications, and events. Assign roles: donor communication, online giving, progress tracking. Meet regularly to assess progress and adjust strategy.

08

Celebrate milestones and thank donors

Celebrate progress publicly when you reach part of a goal, and express gratitude with thank-you messages, events, or impact updates. Reporting back on how funds were used builds trust and encourages ongoing giving.

09

Plan for sustainability

Build a reserve fund for lean months or unexpected costs, consider partnerships or grants to supplement individual giving, and review goals periodically—quarterly or annually—based on actual giving trends.

10

Ground everything in faith

Pray over your financial goals. Fundraising is a spiritual exercise, not just a business task. Teach a theology of trust: God provides, and giving is part of participating in His mission. Use biblical stewardship principles to build a giving culture, not just a campaign.

10

Where to begin

Three steps from this guide

  1. Write a stewardship plan—a decided portion for giving, saving, and spending—and give the first fruits rather than the leftovers.
  2. Set a financial goal tied to an evangelism aim in the goal planner: what do you want to accomplish with the funds?
  3. If you lead, name the one dependency in your ministry you would like to reduce this year, and take the first step toward local ownership.
